### Theorem 5.32

Let $S$ and $T$ be sets. Then $\mathcal{P}(S) \cap \mathcal{P}(T) = \mathcal{P}(S \cap T)$.

*Proof.*

Denote $A = \mathcal{P}(S) \cap \mathcal{P}(T)$ and $B = \mathcal{P}(S \cap T)$.

$(\subseteq)$ Let $x$ be an arbitrary set in $A$. 

By the **Definition of Intersection**, we know $x \in \mathcal{P}(S), \mathcal{P}(T)$. 

By the **Definition of a Power Set**, we know all elements of $x$ are in both $S$ and $T$.

Now, by the **Definition of a Subset**, $x \subseteq (S \cap T)$. Therefore, $x \in \mathcal{P}(S \cap T)$.

Since $x$ was arbitrary, we know $A \subseteq B$.

$(\supseteq)$ Let $y$ be an arbitrary set in $B$.

By the **Definition of a Power Set**, we know $y \subseteq (S \cap T)$.

Hence, all elements of $y$ are in both $S$ and $T$. Thus, $y \subseteq S, y \subseteq T$.

Now, $y \in \mathcal{P}(S), \mathcal{P}(T)$. So, by the **Definition of Intersection**, then $y \in A$.

Since $y$ was arbitrary, we know $A \supseteq B$.

Since $A$ is a superset and subset of $B$, our **Definition of Set Equality** implies $A = B$.

$\blacksquare$

### Theorem 5.33

Let $S$ and $T$ be sets. Then $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$.

*Proof.*

Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T)$.

By the **Definition of Set Union**, we know $x \in \mathcal{P}(S)$ or $x \in \mathcal{P}(T)$. Without the loss of generality, assume $x \in \mathcal{P}(S)$.

Then, the **Definition of a Power Set** implies $x \subseteq S$.

Since $S \cup T$ contains all elements of $S$, then $\mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$ contains all subsets of $S$. 

So $x \in \mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$.

Since $x$ was arbitrary, we conclude $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$.

$\blacksquare$

### Problem 5.34

Provide a counterexample to show that it is not necessarily true that $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T) = \mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$. This verifies that the converse of Theorem 5.33 is not true in general. Is it ever true that $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T)$ and $\mathcal{P}(S \cup T)$ are equal?

Consider when $S = \{\emptyset\}$ and $T = \{1\}$. 

Now $S \cup T = \{\emptyset, 1\}$, so $\mathcal{P}(S \cup T) = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{1\}, \{\emptyset, 1\}\}$. 

But $\mathcal{P}(\emptyset) = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$ and $\mathcal{P}(1) = \{\emptyset, \{1\}\}$. So their union is $\{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{1\}\}$.

However, if $S$ and $T$ are equal. Then $\mathcal{P}(S) \cup \mathcal{P}(T) = \mathcal{P}(S)$. But, $S \cup T = S$. Then $\mathcal{P}(S \cup T) = \mathcal{P}(S)$.
