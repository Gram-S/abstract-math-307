### Question 3.50

<i>"Is Theorem 3.49 true if we only assume $x, y \in \matbb{Z}$? Give a proof or a counterexample."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

*Proof*.
<!-- begin -->
Assume that $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$. Let $x = 1, y = -1$. Now $-1|1$, which is true but $x \leq y$ is not. So **Theorem 3.49** is not true for all $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$. 

$\blacksquare$

### Theorem 3.51

<i>"Assume that $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$. We have a $4|(m^2 + n^2) \iff$ either $m$ is even or $n$ is even."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

*Proof*.
<!-- begin -->
Assume that $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Let $m = 2$ and $n = 3$. Now $4|(2^2 + 3^2)$ or $4|13$. We know $4|13$ is not true. So the original statement does not hold for $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Therefore, **Theorem 3.51** is false.  

$\blacksquare$

### Theorem 3.53

<i>"If $p$ is prime, then either $p = 2$ or $p$ is odd."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

*Proof*.
<!-- begin -->

Assume that $p$ is prime. For the sake of contradiction assume that $p \not= 2$ and $p$ is even. Now $p = 2k$. Since $k$ cannot be $1$, then $p$ now has a factor of $2$, so $p$ is not prime. Thus, assuming $p$ is not $2$ and even leads to a contradiction. Therefore, if $p$ is prime, then either $p = 2$ or $p$ is odd.

$\blacksquare$

### Theorem 3.55

<i>"There are an infinite amount of prime numbers."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

For the sake of contradiction, assume there are a finite amount of prime numbers $p_1, p_2, ... , p_x$. Now assume there exists an integer $n = p_1 * p_2 * ... * p_x$. Consider $n + 1$, which cannot be written as a unique product of primes, since there is no product of primes greater than $n$. Thus, assuming there are a finite amount of prime numbers contradicts **Fact 3.54**. Therefore, the must be an infinite amount of prime numbers.   

$\blacksquare$
