### Theorem 3.51

<i>"Assume that $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$. We have a $4|(m^2 + n^2) \iff$ $m, n$ are even."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

*Proof*.
<!-- begin -->
$\implies$ Assume that $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $4|(m^2 + n^2)$. By **Def 2.11**, $m^2 + n^2 = 4k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. For the sake of contradiction and without the loss of generality, assume that $m$ is odd. Then $m^2$ is odd by **Theorem 3.35**. Using **Def 2.2** we can show the sum of an odd integer and another integer is odd. But **Def 2.1** implies $4k$ is always even. So $m^2 + n^2$ equals an even integer, which is a contradiction. Thus, assuming either $m$ or $n$ is odd leads to a contradiction. Therefore, if $4|(m^2 + n^2)$ then both $m, n$ are even. 

$\blacksquare$

### Theorem 3.55

<i>"There are an infinite amount of prime numbers."</i> <!-- Original claim -->

<!-- We know that n+1 is the combination of two products less than n, otherwise n+1 is less than n -->

For the sake of contradiction, assume there is a finite amount of prime numbers. Then let $n$ be the product of all prime numbers. Note there are no prime numbers greater than $n$. 

Consider $n + 1$. **Fact 3.54** states $n + 1$ has a prime factorization. Write this prime factorization in the form $xy$, for integers $x, y \leq n$, where $x$ and $y$ are non-overlapping parts of $n + 1$'s prime factorization.  

Note that neither $x$ nor $y$ can equal $1$, otherwise $n + 1$ is prime. 

Now, with the loss of generality, consider $n + 1 = xy$, or $\frac{n + 1}{y} = x$. Then $n + 1$, and $y$ share no common factors other than $1$, so $\frac{n + 1}{y}$ cannot be an integer. Thus, $x$ is not an integer, which is a contradiction. 

Thus, assuming there is a finite amount of prime numbers leads to a contradiction. Therefore, there are an infinite amount of prime numbers. 

$\blacksquare$
